# Findings and qualifications

This file concerns the supplied published article by Ünlü and Yalçın, DOI `10.1112/blms/bdq072`. Page numbers are the printed journal pages. See [README.md](README.md) for every coded test, scope limits, and reproduction.

## Exact algebra agrees with the five-stage construction

The multiplication printed on p. 988 agrees with CayleyKernel's basis multiplication at every basis pair through the sedenions. An independently recursive oracle checks the convention; no basis permutation is necessary. `twist()` uses mathematical indices, while `basis()` uses signed one-based labels.

The important distinction is that the sedenions contain zero divisors, but an element embedded from a **strictly earlier** algebra does not acquire an annihilator in the five-stage chain. The full matrix coefficient test establishes

`L_a^T L_a = |a|^2 I`

for each earlier/later inclusion among `R,C,H,O,S`. In particular, for arbitrary embedded octonions `a` and arbitrary sedenions `b`, `|ab|^2=|a|^2|b|^2`. One can also see this from `(a,0)(c,d)=(ac,da)` and octonion norm composition. For a nonzero tensor vector, a single nonzero row supplies injectivity of the coefficient map. Thus the zero-annihilator part of uniformity is certified beyond finite samples.

The exact seeded frames have the full expected **joint** ranks. At fixity four, one tested frame has four blocks of dimension 16 with total rank 64 in a fixed tensor space of dimension 64; there is no nonzero fixed final vector. These frame checks are finite examples, unlike the preceding coefficient identity.

Appending a sixth stage naively fails the constant-annihilator test: a sedenion zero divisor embedded in dimension 32 still has a nonzero annihilator, while the identity does not. This agrees with the authors' explanation that their recipe encounters a division-algebra restriction.

## Section 2: the extra imaginary constraint matters

On p. 986 the authors explain why replacing complex orthogonality with only a real pair inner product would lose freeness. Our exact example uses a representation of `C2` fixing real row directions `u0,u1` and negating two others:

- `v1=u0`;
- `v2=u1` in the complex stage;
- `v3=u1*i` in the quaternionic stage.

All three have unit norm and are fixed. The weaker real inner product of `v2` with `v3` is zero, but the inner product of `v2*i` with `v3` is 1. The correct constraints reject precisely this false fixed point.

## Section 3: wording and hypotheses that need care

### The section heading names the wrong theorem

The heading on p. 986 says “Proof of Theorem 1.1”. The section develops the general construction and explicitly proves **Theorem 1.2** on pp. 988–989. The documentation follows the actual mathematical content.

### Pairwise intersections alone do not certify a direct sum

Definition 3.2 on p. 987 uses pairwise zero intersections of generated subspaces. Lemma 3.3 then describes the sum as a Whitney direct sum. For general linear subspaces, pairwise zero intersection does not imply joint independence: the lines generated by `(1,0)`, `(0,1)`, and `(1,1)` are the elementary example.

This diagnostic is not a full counterexample to the lemma under all its filtration hypotheses. It does mean that this review cannot claim the general direct-sum conclusion solely from pairwise rank tests. The actual Cayley–Dickson tests therefore check the rank of **all** generated blocks together. All tested admissible frames satisfy the full expected rank. A general argument for every frame and every abstract filtration is not supplied by this code.

Likewise, the discussion of free modules over earlier algebras must respect the nonassociative warning at the beginning of Section 3. Explicit associator tests ensure that our implementation never silently replaces `(v*a)*b` with `v*(a*b)` in the octonion/sedenion stages.

### Vector rank and sphere dimension differ by one

On p. 989 the Whitney-sum paragraph calls `E(q)` a real vector bundle of dimension `q(16n-64)-1`. The original complement rank is `16n-64`; a `q`-fold Whitney sum has vector rank **`q(16n-64)`**. Its unit sphere fibre has dimension **`q(16n-64)-1`**. The following stable-range inequality uses the sphere dimension, so that inequality and the conclusion that `q=2` suffices are consistent with the corrected rank.

The four unmodified thresholds in equation (3.1) are `n>=2`, `n>=5`, `n>=12`, and `n>=27`. At the theorem's lower bound `n=12`, two copies at the final stage give sphere dimensions

`11, 21, 39, 71, 255`.

These are conditional product dimensions from the construction; the code does not produce a smooth product trivialization.

### Corollary 1.3: raw coordinate selection is not necessarily a Stiefel frame

The first sentence on p. 990 asserts the existence of indices selecting one real coordinate vector from each stage to give a point of `V5(V)`. Literally, this is stronger than what the constraints ensure. Even after normalizing individual selected vectors, they need not be mutually orthogonal.

Write `u0,...,u4` for mutually orthonormal real coordinate directions. The following are admissible nonzero rays in the five successive tensor spaces:

```
v1 = u0
v2 = u1 + u2*i
v3 = (u1+u2) + (u1-u2)*i
v4 = u3
v5 = u4
```

Divide each ray by its norm to obtain a point of `W5`. The complex orthogonality of stages 2 and 3 follows from

```
u1·(u1+u2) + u2·(u1-u2) = 1-1 = 0
-u2·(u1+u2) + u1·(u1-u2) = -1+1 = 0.
```

The code also checks every higher-algebra right-multiplication constraint, not just these two equations. Yet either nonzero real component of `v2` has nonzero inner product with either nonzero component of `v3`:

```
u1·(u1+u2)=1    u1·(u1-u2)=1
u2·(u1+u2)=1    u2·(u1-u2)=-1.
```

There are four nonzero raw selections, and none is orthogonal. This remains true after the required unit normalization. To place the example in the corollary's setting, take `V=R^13` and let the nonidentity element of `C2` act as `diag(I5,-I8)`. This is faithful, oriented, has fixity 5, and fixes every component of the example.

What the argument needs is the existence of an invariant orthonormal five-frame, rather than literal raw selection. All real components here span five dimensions. Gram–Schmidt, followed by normalization, produces five fixed orthonormal vectors. Since an isotropy subgroup fixes every input component, it also fixes every real linear combination used in Gram–Schmidt. The code supplies this correction explicitly **for the example** and for another seeded frame. It does not prove the required coordinate-rank assertion for all admissible frames or implement the remaining periodic-cohomology/finite-complex argument.

The passing test records a failure of the literal coordinate-selection sentence, together with a local repair. It is not evidence that the corollary itself is false.
